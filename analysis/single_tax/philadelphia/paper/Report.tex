\begin{abstract}
\noindent Philadelphia collects \StxLaborB{} a year from taxes on labor and \StxCapitalB{}
from taxes on capital. Could a tax on land rent replace them? This report answers a narrower
and more answerable question: how much of each abolished tax would have to reappear as land
rent --- a quantity we write $\kappa$ --- for full land-rent capture to fund the abolition
while holding the City and School District whole. Abolishing the Wage \& Earnings Tax and the
entire tax on structures breaks even at $\kappa$ of \StxBThreeKappaOpa{} on the City
assessor's land values, but \StxBThreeKappaLycd{} on the alternative published land surface.
That spread is the finding --- not because the surface moves the answer more than every other
input (the swept discount rate moves it more), but because it is the one input that is not the
analyst's to choose: a disagreement between two published assessments of the same parcels,
\StxSurfaceGapPct{} apart in aggregate and uneven across the city. For the largest reform
bundles the break-even threshold falls inside the range that published capitalization evidence
brackets, so the literature is consistent with the reform both succeeding and failing ---
though for the full program on the assessor's surface, the succeeding half of that range rests
on a modeled open-city ceiling. And a companion sales study shows the disagreement is not
beyond evidence: on vacant land --- the majority of the gap, and the one class where a sale
price reveals the land value directly --- \emph{both} surfaces fail, in opposite directions.
Philadelphia cannot price this reform more precisely than it can assess its land, and its own
sales records already measure how far short that falls.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

Proposals to shift Philadelphia's tax burden onto land value are usually argued at the level
of principle: land is fixed in supply, so taxing it does not discourage the thing being taxed.
The harder question is arithmetic. Philadelphia raises roughly \StxLaborB{} a year from the
Wage \& Earnings Tax and its relatives, and \StxCapitalB{} from taxes that fall on capital ---
the tax on structures, the Business Income \& Receipts Tax, the Realty Transfer Tax, and
others. A land tax that replaced them would have to raise the same money out of a base that,
today, generates \StxLandTaxB{} a year in land-portion property tax.

The gap looks impossible until you notice what abolition does to land rent. A tax on wages
makes a city a worse place to live and work; abolishing it makes the city more attractive, and
in a city with a fixed supply of land, part of that gain is bid into land rents. The same
holds for a tax on buildings. This is the mechanism behind the century-old claim, sometimes
called ATCOR --- All Taxes Come Out Of Rents --- that the land base grows as other taxes are
removed.

Whether it grows \emph{enough} is an empirical question, and this report does not pretend to
settle it. Instead it does something more useful for a decision-maker: it computes, exactly,
how much of that effect the reform would need. We call that break-even share $\kappa^*$. If
$\kappa^*$ is 0.5, then half of every dollar of abolished tax would have to reappear as land % noqa: hardcode -- illustrative threshold, not a result
rent for the program to fund itself. A reader who believes capitalization is stronger than
that should support the reform on these numbers; a reader who believes it is weaker should
not. The point of the exercise is to make the disagreement precise.

\section{Data and methods}

\paragraph{The ledger.} We assemble every City and School District tax that the program would
abolish, from the City's Quarterly City Manager's Report and School District sources, at
\StxLedgerLines{} lines with a source and a vintage attached to each
(Table~\ref{tab:ledger}). Two lines --- the School Income Tax and the Business Use \&
Occupancy Tax --- are secondary-source estimates rather than verified figures, and are flagged
as such wherever they appear. Figure~\ref{fig:ledger} shows the totals.

The land-portion property tax is treated differently from the rest. It is \emph{absorbed},
not abolished: the City and School District must still be made whole for the
\StxLandTaxB{} a year they currently collect on land, so it enters the funding target for
every bundle and is never counted as a tax the reform eliminates.

\paragraph{The break-even calculation.} For a bundle $B$ of abolished taxes with annual
receipts $T_j$, the reform must raise $\text{Target} = \sum_{j \in B} T_j + T_{\text{land}}$.
The rent levy supplies $\varphi(1-h)\left(R_0 + \sum_j \kappa_j T_j\right) + G$, where $R_0$ is
current annual site rent, $\varphi$ is the share of assessed rent actually captured, $h$ is a
haircut for delinquency and abandonment, and $G$ is net-new revenue from pricing roads and
curbs. Setting supply equal to target and solving for a uniform $\kappa$ gives
\[
  \kappa^*(B) \;=\; \frac{\text{Target} - G - \varphi(1-h)R_0}{\varphi(1-h)\sum_{j \in B} T_j}.
\]
$\kappa^*$ is exact, because $\kappa$ enters linearly. A value at or below zero means the
bundle funds itself with no capitalization at all. A value above one means it would need more
rent than the revenue foregone --- possible in principle, since abolishing a distortionary tax
recovers its excess burden as well as its revenue, but demanding.

\paragraph{What is swept, not estimated.} $\kappa$ is a swept parameter throughout. We never
estimate it and then report a single answer; we report the threshold and let the reader supply
their own belief. We sweep \StxSweepRows{} combinations of land surface, rent basis, discount
rate, capture share, road-rent scenario, and haircut. Numbers quoted without qualification are
the central cell: a \StxDiscountRate{} discount rate, full capture ($\varphi = 1$), no haircut,
and no road rent.

One structural feature is worth flagging because it looks like a coincidence in the results:
$\kappa^*$ depends on $\varphi$ and $h$ only through their product $\varphi(1-h)$, so a
capture share of \StxPhiLow{} and a \StxHaircutHighPct{} haircut are the same assumption expressed two ways.

\paragraph{Two published land surfaces.} Computing $R_0$ requires knowing what Philadelphia's
land is worth, and here the exercise runs into a problem that turns out to be the report's main
finding. Two published parcel-level land surfaces exist for the city: the Office of Property
Assessment's own land values, and an independent surface produced by the Land Yield Curve
Dataset (LYCD). They do not agree. We carry both through every calculation rather than
choosing between them.

\paragraph{Bounding $\kappa$ from published evidence.} To ask whether the required $\kappa^*$
is plausible, we bracket each tax family with published capitalization and incidence
estimates (Table~\ref{tab:bounds}). The bounds are deliberately wide, because the literature
is. For the tax on structures the two best-identified studies disagree outright: German
municipal property-tax reforms show near-full pass-through to tenants with landowners bearing
almost nothing, while Philadelphia's own 10-year construction abatement % noqa: hardcode -- statutory term, not a result
shows capitalization of
\StxKappaBuildingHigh{} of its value into new-construction prices. For the wage tax the upper bound
comes from a general-equilibrium counterfactual of \emph{this tax in this city}, and it should
be read as a ceiling rather than a central estimate: it assumes an open city in which all
incidence necessarily falls on landowners. The lower bound for that family is transferred from
a study of state corporate taxes, because no published work estimates the landowner share of a
local wage tax.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The two land surfaces disagree, and not by a constant}

Philadelphia's taxable land generates \StxRentOpa{} a year in site rent on the assessor's
values and \StxRentLycd{} on the LYCD surface --- a difference of \StxSurfaceGapPct{}. That
aggregate gap understates the problem, because the disagreement is not a uniform scaling
factor. Across \StxTractN{} census tracts the median ratio of LYCD to OPA land value is
\StxTractMedianRatio{}, but the 5th percentile is \StxTractPFive{} and the 95th is
\StxTractPNinetyFive{}. In \StxTractShareLycdHigher{} of tracts LYCD assigns more land value;
in the remaining \StxTractShareOpaHigher{} the assessor does.

Figure~\ref{fig:surfacemap} shows where. The disagreement is pervasive rather than patterned
by distance from the core: LYCD assigns more land value in \StxTractShareLycdHigher{} of
tracts, and there is no simple core-to-edge gradient. The divergence is largest through the
mid-city row-house belts (median \StxMidBeltMedianRatio{}) and along the industrial
riverfront, and smallest in the city's outermost tracts (median \StxOuterMedianRatio{}). The
minority of tracts where the assessor assigns more do cluster centrally --- their median
distance from City Hall is \StxBelowOneMedianKm{}~km, against \StxAllMedianKm{}~km for the
city as a whole --- though even the typical Center City tract carries more LYCD than OPA land
value. What matters for policy is that the surfaces disagree about \emph{which} land holds
the value, not only about how much: \StxRankShiftSharePct{} of tracts move more than ten
percentile ranks in their share of the levy between the two surfaces. A reform whose burden
depends on the land surface does not merely become larger or smaller depending on which one
is right; it falls on \emph{different neighborhoods}.

\subsection{What each reform bundle requires}

Table~\ref{tab:bundles} and Figure~\ref{fig:kappastar} give the break-even thresholds. Two
bundles clear on the arithmetic alone. Abolishing the tax on structures alone requires
$\kappa^*$ of \StxBOneKappaOpa{} on the assessor's land and \StxBOneKappaLycd{} on LYCD ---
both negative, meaning the land base already supports it with no capitalization whatsoever.
Abolishing the Wage \& Earnings Tax alone requires \StxBTwoKappaOpa{} on OPA land and
\StxBTwoKappaLycd{} on LYCD, so on the independent surface that bundle, too, funds itself
outright.

The bundle that matters most is the third: abolish the Wage \& Earnings Tax \emph{and} the
entire tax on structures, absorb the land tax, and fund all of it from site rent. That is a
\StxBThreeTargetB{} annual target, and it requires $\kappa^*$ of \StxBThreeKappaOpa{} on the
assessor's land surface against \StxBThreeKappaLycd{} on LYCD. The full program --- adding
BIRT, the Net Profits Tax, the School Income Tax, Use \& Occupancy, and the Realty Transfer
Tax --- requires \StxBFiveKappaOpa{} and \StxBFiveKappaLycd{} respectively.

\subsection{The published evidence does not discriminate}

Figure~\ref{fig:kappastar} plots those thresholds against the range that published
capitalization estimates support. Of the \StxCellsTotal{} bundle-by-surface combinations,
\StxCellsBelowBand{} fall below the published range --- they pencil more easily than even the
least favorable published estimate would require --- and \StxCellsAboveBand{} fall above it.
The remaining \StxCellsInsideBand{}, which include every one of the three largest bundles on
both land surfaces, fall \emph{inside} it.

That is the honest result, and it is a weak one: for the reforms that matter most, the
empirical literature is consistent with the program funding itself and with it falling
substantially short. The band is wide because the evidence is genuinely contested, not because
we declined to look. The single widest contributor is the tax on structures, where the two
best-identified studies disagree from one end of the range to the other.

Two qualifications keep this from being read as more settled than it is. The counts above are
stated at the central cell and are not invariant to it: across the \StxOpPointsTotal{}
operating points the sweep covers, \StxOpPointsAnyAbove{} put at least one bundle \emph{above}
the band --- the failure side --- under less favorable combinations of discount rate, capture,
and haircut. And for the two largest bundles on the assessor's surface, the
``consistent-with-succeeding'' half of the verdict rests on the wage family's upper bound,
which is a modeled open-city ceiling rather than an estimate
(Table~\ref{tab:bounds}); the wage-plus-structures bundle is the one whose inside-the-band
standing survives on point estimates alone.

\subsection{What moves the answer}

Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} varies each input in turn for the third bundle. Ranked by the
range each produces: the discount rate moves $\kappa^*$ by \StxSpanDiscountOpa{} on the
assessor's surface (\StxSpanDiscountLycd{} on LYCD) across its swept range; the
capture-and-haircut knob --- one knob, since $\kappa^*$ depends only on the product
$\varphi(1-h)$ --- moves it by \StxSpanCapture{} across its grid; the land surface moves it by
\StxSpanSurface{}; road rent by \StxSpanRoadRent{}. The land surface is not the largest of
these, and this report should not be read as claiming it is.

What sets the land surface apart is not its size but its kind. The discount-rate and
capture ranges are the analyst's to choose: sweep them narrower --- over the
\StxDiscountLowPct{}--\StxDiscountHighPct{} range we chose, or a tighter one --- and their
spans shrink by construction. The land-surface gap is not a choice. It is a disagreement
between two published assessments of the same parcels, and the only way to shrink it is to
find out which assessment is wrong. The next section shows that, for the largest single
component of the gap, the city's own sales records answer that question --- against both
surfaces.

\subsection{Where the truth is observable, both surfaces fail}

The surface disagreement is not beyond evidence. For a bare lot, the sale price \emph{is} the
land price --- the thing that makes land valuation hard everywhere else, separating land from
structure, does not apply. A companion study in this project
(\texttt{docs/VACANT\_LAND\_VALUATION.md}, reproducible from the city's recorded sales) runs
that test on \StxVacantN{} bare-land sales. In aggregate, the assessor carries vacant land at
\StxVacantOpaAgg{} its sale value; LYCD carries it at \StxVacantLycdAgg{}. The study
attributes the majority of the citywide surface gap to this one property class, and finds that
correcting it alone would cut the two surfaces' aggregate disagreement roughly in half ---
the surfaces bracket the truth from opposite sides, and two independent corrections converge.

Nor does either surface merely mis-level vacant land; both scatter it. On the same sales, the
coefficient of dispersion is \StxVacantOpaCod{} for the assessor and \StxVacantLycdCod{} for
LYCD, against a professional (IAAO) standard of 20 % noqa: hardcode -- fixed professional standard, not a result
--- cheap lots over-assessed severalfold, expensive lots under-assessed by half. For this
report that cuts two ways. It means the headline disagreement is largely a measurable,
correctable assessment failure rather than an irreducible unknown. And it means that on the
property class a land tax most needs to reach, \emph{neither} published surface currently
supports accurate billing.

\section{Caveats}

\paragraph{This is a static ledger.} No behavioral response is modeled anywhere. Migration,
agglomeration, changes in the built stock, and labor-supply effects are all absent. This is
the point of the design --- $\kappa$ is precisely the channel through which those responses
would arrive, and it is swept rather than assumed --- but it means the report cannot say what
\emph{would} happen, only what would have to be true.

\paragraph{The published bounds are for marginal changes.} Every estimate behind
Table~\ref{tab:bounds} concerns a small change to a tax that continues to exist. This program
abolishes several taxes at once, at full land-rent capture. Nothing in the literature speaks to
whether a marginal capitalization rate survives at that scale, and the bounds should be read as
bracketing the marginal case only.

\paragraph{The wage family is the weakest link.} It is also the largest, at
\StxBThreeWageSharePct{} of the third bundle and \StxBFiveWageSharePct{} of the full program.
Its upper bound is a modeled ceiling that assumes perfect mobility, and its lower bound is
transferred from a different tax in a setting without Philadelphia's large non-resident
commuter base. Nothing published estimates the interior of that range.

\paragraph{Full capture is a strong assumption.} Setting $\varphi = 1$ means collecting every
dollar of assessed site rent. Where assessment is wrong, full capture collects the error too;
the haircut sweep is a crude stand-in for that and for outright abandonment, not a model of it.

\paragraph{Road and curb rents are conservative.} The central road-rent figure of
\StxGCentralM{} a year comes from an external cordon-pricing model and is carried as a modeled
input, never a verified one. Curb revenue is deliberately excluded: Philadelphia already
directs its on-street parking revenue to the City and the School District, so counting it again
would let one dollar fund both today's spending and the reform.

\section{What follows}

The result that should change a reader's mind is not any single $\kappa^*$. It is that the
answer swings by \StxSpanSurface{} depending on which published land surface you believe ---
and that where the disagreement can be tested against sales, both surfaces fail. The ledger
cannot adjudicate between them; the city's own recorded sales, on the one property class where
land value is directly observable, partially can, and they convict both.

Philadelphia is being asked to consider a reform whose entire logic runs through the value of
its land, and the city does not have a settled measurement of what its land is worth. The two
published answers differ by \StxSurfaceGapPct{} in aggregate, disagree about which
neighborhoods hold the value, and --- where they can be checked --- are wrong in opposite
directions, at dispersion several times the professional standard. Before the city can price a
land-value tax, it has to be able to assess land. Unlike the capitalization question, that
failure is already measured, already localized to a property class, and addressable with
records the city already collects.

That is a smaller ask than a single tax, and a more urgent one. It is also a prerequisite: any
version of this reform, at any ambition, inherits whichever land surface the assessor produces.
Getting that measurement right is not a technical preliminary to the debate about land value
taxation. On the evidence here, it \emph{is} the debate.
